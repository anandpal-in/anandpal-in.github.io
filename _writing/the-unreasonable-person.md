I often reflect on a simple but uncomfortable question: *what slows social and intellectual progress the most?* The answer I return to, again and again, is political correctness.

Not a lack of intelligence. Not a lack of resources. More often, it is the desire to fit in.

Political correctness, at its core, is not about truth. It is about adaptation. When ten people repeat something incorrect with confidence, it becomes safer to agree than to question. Conformity offers comfort, acceptance, and a sense of security. Dissent offers uncertainty, isolation, and risk.

Most people, understandably, choose comfort. Yet this very choice is what quietly halts progress. When everyone adapts to a flawed consensus, a society or an organization begins to live inside a shared illusion. No one wants to be the first to say, “This may be wrong.”

Progress, however, has never emerged from a state of comfort. It has always required someone willing to be uncomfortable.

## The reasonable and the unreasonable

George Bernard Shaw captured this truth with remarkable clarity:

> The reasonable man adapts himself to the world; the unreasonable one persists in trying to adapt the world to himself. Therefore all progress depends on the unreasonable man.

Most people are reasonable. They adapt. They adjust their views to match the room they are in. That is how societies remain calm, but also how they stagnate.

The “unreasonable” person is not reckless or arrogant. They are unwilling to trade truth for acceptance. A reasonable person adapts because adaptation helps them climb existing ladders. An unreasonable person asks whether the ladder is leaning against the right wall.

## A pattern history keeps repeating

Galileo Galilei challenged the geocentric worldview at a time when political, religious, and social correctness demanded silence. He was not wrong; he was early. And being early is often mistaken for being unreasonable.

Centuries later, classical physics stood as a near-perfect framework of knowledge. Then came ideas that disturbed its foundations: uncertainty, probability, and indeterminism. The early architects of quantum theory were not celebrated for their politeness; they were tolerated because reality eventually validated their ideas.

The same pattern appears across disciplines and eras:

- Alan Turing challenged conventional ideas of what intelligence is.
- Linus Torvalds championed open-source software, which now powers much of the world’s digital infrastructure.
- Elon Musk insisted that reusable rockets were viable, reshaping the space industry.
- Quantum computing began not as an improvement on classical machines, but as a refusal to accept that computation had already found its final form.

None of these ideas emerged from consensus. They emerged from dissent.

## When institutions reward correctness over courage

What is worrying today is that the institutions meant to protect dissent, such as universities, academic publishing, and journalism, increasingly reward correctness over courage. Instead of creating safe spaces for uncomfortable ideas, they often teach careful conformity. Not because people are malicious, but because systems quietly incentivize agreement.

Progress does not require everyone to be unreasonable. It requires that *someone* be allowed to be. It requires environments where disagreement is not treated as disloyalty, and where asking “Are we sure?” is seen as responsible rather than rebellious.

If history teaches us anything, it is this: **truth has rarely been politically correct at birth.**

Perhaps the real measure of a healthy society or institution is not how well it agrees, but how well it listens to the voice that dares to disagree. Almost everything we now call common sense once appeared unreasonable. Progress has never come from quiet adaptation, but from those willing to stand alone. That is why we owe so much of our progress to the “unreasonable” person.
