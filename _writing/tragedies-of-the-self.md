> What is common to the greatest number gets the least amount of care.
>
> — Aristotle, *Politics*

We are so occupied with our short-term needs that we rarely consider the impact of our actions on society, culture, health, the environment, and future generations. We take natural resources, human resources, freedom, technology, and much else for granted.

The first to suffer from our self-centred actions are the *commons*: resources that we share collectively and that everyone needs for society to flourish. This is the **tragedy of the commons**: a situation in which individuals overuse a shared, finite resource without considering its long-term impact, ultimately harming the environment and the community as a whole. The tragedy unfolds when many people, each acting in their own interest, overexploit a common resource. Unsustainable use leads to depletion, ecosystem damage, and eventually the collapse of the entire system.

In such situations, individuals prioritize their immediate needs over the well-being of the larger system, forgetting a fundamental rule:

**Optimizing for oneself in the short term isn’t beneficial for anyone in the long term.**

## Everywhere we look

Examples are all around us: the overuse of antibiotics; coal-based power plants producing cheap electricity; the overuse of vehicles, leading to congestion and environmental degradation; the excessive use of plastic. Groundwater depletion from intensive agriculture threatens water supplies in drought-prone regions, while fast fashion and the constant demand for new electronic gadgets contribute massively to pollution. There are hundreds of such examples.

These tragedies do not only occur on a global scale. You can find them within your family or workplace too, wherever people misuse shared resources, such as office supplies or library materials, for short-term individual benefit. In the end, management responds with new regulations that complicate everyone’s life, including the lives of those responsible for the misuse.

Thus, the tragedies of the commons become tragedies of the self.

## The commons within

There is a second, quieter version of this tragedy. Each of us also manages a personal commons: our attention, our health, our time, and our relationships. These are finite resources shared between our present and future selves. Every day we make small decisions that serve the present at the expense of the future: another hour of scrolling, another skipped night of sleep, another postponed conversation. No single choice feels harmful, just as no single fisherman empties the sea. But together they deplete the resources our future self depends on. This is the tragedy of the self.

## Responsibility and trust

To avoid these tragedies, we must be mindful of our actions and their social, political, and ecological consequences. A sustainable, shared environment requires us to respect others’ space and to recognize that resources belong to everyone. We need to think beyond short-term personal gain and consider the long-term good of the commons.

**What is not beneficial for all cannot be truly beneficial for any individual.**

Leaders have a responsibility to promote practices that serve the common good, rather than rewarding those who pursue short-term self-interest. Unfortunately, this mindset has not gained much ground, largely because of a lack of trust. When individuals believe that others will not act in the group’s interest, they find it easy to rationalize their own selfish behaviour.

The ecologist Garrett Hardin, who popularized the “tragedy of the commons” in 1968, concluded that shared resources must be either privatized or regulated. But Elinor Ostrom, who received the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences, showed that communities often manage commons successfully on their own, through trust, shared rules, and mutual accountability. Her work supports a simple idea: where people trust one another, heavy regulation becomes unnecessary.

A society or group in which people are mindful of their actions, and trust that others are doing the same, can thrive without extensive regulation, making life simpler and more sustainable.

Simplicity leads to happiness, and responsibility towards shared spaces ensures a better world for us all.
