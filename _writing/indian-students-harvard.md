Recent data from Harvard University points to a significant shift in global student mobility. In Fall 2025, Harvard recorded its highest-ever proportion of international students, at 28%. Yet during the same period, Indian student enrolment across its schools declined sharply, by nearly 31%, from 788 students to 545.

This trend contrasts with developments elsewhere. Enrolment from China rose to 1,452 students, while South Korean student numbers grew by approximately 8.7%. Chinese students remain the largest international cohort at Harvard. This divergence suggests that the issue is not a decline in global demand, but a recalibration of student choices shaped by policy signals and perceptions of risk.

## What is driving the decline

Several factors appear to be contributing:

- **Policy and visa uncertainty.** Stricter visa processing, longer timelines, and repeated public signals questioning international student access have created uncertainty. Even when legal challenges have gone in favour of universities, the lack of policy predictability has encouraged a “wait and watch” approach among Indian applicants.
- **Funding and research concerns.** Temporary freezes on federal research funding have affected assistantships and research opportunities, a particular concern for Indian graduate students, for whom funding security is a key consideration.
- **Return on investment.** Indian students are increasingly weighing cost, post-study work options, and long-term stability. Canada, Australia, the UK, and parts of Europe offer clearer, more predictable pathways to work permits and residency, which shapes decisions in a high-cost, high-risk environment.
- **Global competition for talent.** While Indian enrolment has softened, growth from China and South Korea shows that student mobility is becoming more differentiated, shaped by national strategies, institutional pipelines, and confidence in long-term outcomes.

## What India should do

Indian universities and policymakers need a more strategic response:

- Expanding joint, dual-degree, and twinning programmes with global partners
- Strengthening co-supervised research and faculty exchange
- Attracting international students to India through high-quality academic programmes and research ecosystems
- Reducing dependence on a narrow set of destination countries

Global talent flows are highly responsive to policy signals. For Indian universities, this is a timely opportunity to reposition themselves, not only as sources of talent, but as equal partners and destinations in a more balanced and resilient global higher education landscape.
