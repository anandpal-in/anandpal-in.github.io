There is a recurring pattern in India’s higher education policy that deserves serious reflection.

Over the past three decades, the emphasis has repeatedly shifted: from publishing more research papers, to filing more patents, and now to promoting product-based PhDs. Each of these priorities has merit. The concern is not the goals themselves, but the tendency to adopt global trends without first asking whether they align with India’s academic ecosystem, industrial capacity, and long-term national priorities.

## Three decades, three targets

In the 2000s, promotions came to depend on publication scores, and paper counts rose rapidly, along with predatory journals, which later forced regulators to curate lists of approved journals. Rankings then began rewarding patents, and filings rose, though far fewer have been licensed or turned into real products. Now the emphasis is shifting to products. Each time, institutions responded exactly as the incentives asked. And each time, the measure improved while the underlying capability changed far less.

## The cost of changing direction

Frequent changes in direction create uncertainty for universities, for researchers, and most of all for young scholars, who are expected to reshape their careers around the latest policy narrative. Universities should not become experimental grounds for ever-changing policy fashions.

What India needs is not the next global buzzword, but a clear, original, and long-term vision for research and higher education, one that reflects our own strengths, challenges, and aspirations. Policies should evolve through evidence, self-reflection, and continuity, rather than in reaction to what other countries are doing.

The real question is not whether we should publish papers, file patents, or build products. It is whether our policies are driven by a coherent national strategy or by changing international trends.

## What a coherent strategy would look like

A coherent strategy would start from the problems India most needs to solve, in health, energy, water, and agriculture, and ask which mix of papers, patents, and products serves each. It would hold research priorities steady for at least a decade, and evaluate researchers on the significance of their work rather than on whichever output is currently fashionable.

**Sustainable excellence is built through consistency, not constant shifts in direction.**
