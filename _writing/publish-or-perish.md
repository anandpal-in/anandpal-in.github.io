When publications become the measure of success, education risks losing sight of its purpose.

Last week, IEEE retracted at least 1,417 conference papers in a single day, the largest batch of papers ever retracted at once by a scholarly publisher. The papers had been linked to paper mills: businesses that advertise authorship slots on Telegram, WhatsApp and Facebook, with first-author positions offered for as little as $43. Of the 109 IEEE-sponsored conferences with five or more flagged papers, 95 were held in India.

It is tempting to treat this as a problem of fraud detection. It is not. It is a problem of system we build. Someone bought those authorship slots because a degree, a job or a promotion depended on them. Somewhere, a student learned that a paper is something you *acquire*, not something you *earn by understanding*. That lesson was taught to them, not by any single teacher, but by the system as a whole.

## What is a university for?

Every education system rests on an answer, stated or unstated, to a simple question: what are we trying to make of a person? 

The modern research system in the academic institutions was built on a clear answer. When Wilhelm von Humboldt helped found the University of Berlin in 1810, the guiding idea was the *unity of research and teaching*. Research and teaching were not two separate jobs. Research was a way of learning, and the professor was a fellow inquirer who showed students how knowledge is made, questioned and corrected. The goal was the formation of a mind, not the production of an output.

Seen this way, a publication was meant to be a by-product of that formation: the visible trace of a genuine question pursued honestly. Today it has become the goal itself. **Publication is no longer a by-product of research; it is treated as research.** And once the paper becomes the purpose, the student becomes a means of producing it.

## From formation to production

The language we use gives us away. We speak of faculty “producing” PhDs, of students as “manpower” on a project, of research groups by their “output”. A doctoral programme, which should be an apprenticeship in thinking, begins to look like a production line, with papers as the units of output and students as the labour.

This changes what happens between a teacher and a student. A good mentor asks: *what does this student need to struggle with in order to grow?* A metric-driven mentor asks: *what can this student publish by the next appraisal?* The first question sometimes leads to a year spent on a problem that does not work out, a year in which the student learns more about science than any paper could record. The second question rarely allows that year.

Teaching suffers in the same way. Faculty appraisals reward papers, grants and citations, while the hours spent preparing a lecture, redesigning a course or sitting with a struggling student leave almost no trace. Teaching, nurturing curiosity and inspiring the next generation of thinkers is a fundamental responsibility of faculty. When it counts for little, it slowly becomes the work people do after the “real” work is finished.

## The hidden curriculum

Students do not learn only from what we teach. They learn, perhaps more deeply, from what we celebrate.

I have watched this change between my PhD days and now. Back then, when a speaker was introduced at a seminar or conference, the introduction was about their contribution: the problem they had worked on and what they had shown. Today, we introduce people by the number of papers they have published, the number of PhD students they have produced and their h-index, rather than by what they have contributed to solving a problem.

Every such introduction is a lesson. It tells the young people in the audience what a successful life in science looks like. We show them that the numbers matter, not the rigor. When numbers carry the value, people naturally follow them. A paper mill is simply the market’s answer to a demand that our institutions created.

The economist Charles Goodhart’s observation, as the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern later phrased it, applies with full force: *when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.* Publication counts were once a rough sign of an active, curious mind. Made into a target, they reward the appearance of a curious mind instead.

## Curiosity cannot be counted

The deepest goods of education are the hardest to measure: curiosity, intellectual honesty, the patience to stay with a difficult problem, the courage to say “I was wrong.” None of these appears in an h-index. A system that sees only what it can count will, over time, value only what it can count, and its students will learn to do the same.

Paulo Freire warned against “banking” education, in which knowledge is deposited into students rather than discovered with them. The race to publish creates a banking model of research: papers are deposited into databases, counted and drawn upon at the time of hiring and promotion, while the act of understanding that should lie behind them becomes optional. I have written before about a similar drift towards [designing education for satisfaction rather than understanding](/writing/satisfaction-or-understanding/). The race for publications is the same drift at the level of research.

## A question worth asking

Rankings, citation metrics and measurable achievements have brought valuable visibility to scientific work. They should complement the pursuit of knowledge, not replace it. The problem lies in what we choose to reward, because what we incentivize shapes what we produce, and, more importantly, *who* we produce.

So perhaps it is time to ask a fundamental question: **are our academic systems designed to nurture original thinking, intellectual honesty and meaningful contributions, or do they teach our students that research outputs matter more than knowledge itself?**

Retractions treat the symptom. The cure lies in how we evaluate, introduce, hire and promote people, and in whether we are willing to value a teacher who forms ten careful thinkers as highly as one who publishes a hundred papers.

The purpose of science is not merely to produce more papers. The purpose of education is not merely to produce more graduates. Both exist to advance reliable knowledge, to form minds capable of advancing it further and to make a meaningful difference to society.
