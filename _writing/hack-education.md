Recently, a student visited me seeking guidance for a career in physics. His curiosity was genuine, yet as our discussion unfolded, I realized he lacked some of the very fundamentals of the subject.

I asked what had sparked his interest in physics. His reply: “I followed some lecture series on YouTube.”

Inspiring, yes, but incomplete.

To guide him, I suggested starting with *Fundamentals of Physics* by Halliday and Resnick, a book that has been a gold standard of physics education worldwide for decades. With its clear explanations, rigorous problem sets, and comprehensive coverage, it has shaped generations of physicists and engineers.

To my surprise, he had never heard of it, despite spending **four years** at one of India’s most reputed IIT-JEE coaching institutes.

This made me pause. Later, when I asked a larger group of students the same question, **only 6–7% had heard of the book**, even though almost all of them had spent significant time in coaching centres.

## Why coaching optimizes for the wrong thing

Coaching institutes are not failing at their task; they are succeeding at it. Their goal is a rank in a fixed-format, time-bound examination, and the most efficient route to a rank is pattern recognition, shortcuts, and drilling, not understanding. A textbook like Halliday and Resnick is slow. It asks students to build intuition from first principles. In a race measured in marks per minute, depth looks like inefficiency.

## The questions it raises

In our pursuit of cracking competitive exams, are we sidelining the very foundations of education: critical thinking, logical reasoning, creativity, problem-solving, and a genuine curiosity to explore?

Are we encouraging shortcuts, formula memorization, and “hack-based” learning at the expense of depth and of the *why* behind the subject?

YouTube lectures can inspire and can be useful, but they cannot replace books. At some point, one has to sit down, struggle with problems, and work through the rigour. That is where real learning happens.

## What we can do

Universities can rebuild what coaching skips. First-year courses can prioritize conceptual understanding over coverage. Assessment can reward explanation, not only answers. Students can be encouraged to read original texts, not just notes. As Richard Feynman wrote on his blackboard: *“What I cannot create, I do not understand.”* The goal of education is not to recognize the right answer, but to be able to build it.

The biggest challenge before educators today is this: **how do we bring back a culture of deep learning, in which students grow not just as test-takers, but as thinkers and innovators?**
